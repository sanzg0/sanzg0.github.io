About Me

I am a public finance economist interested in digital markets and data privacy. My research informs policy debates on digital taxation, data privacy, and salary transparency. My job market paper studies how France’s digital services tax affects web traffic to multinational digital platforms.

Before my PhD, I worked as a research assistant at the Federal Reserve Board (2017–2020). I earned my BA in Economics with Honors from Stanford University in 2017.

I have roots in San Juan, Puerto Rico, and Orlando, Florida.

Research

Job market paper & working papers

Job Market Paper

Taxing Multinational Digital Platforms

Abstract

I study the effect of France’s digital services tax on web traffic to multinational digital platforms. Some theoretical models predict that the tax is non-distortionary. I find that it increases traffic to liable advertising-funded sites and reduces traffic to liable e-commerce sites. I classify sites using their corporate groups’ revenue shares from advertising versus intermediation, and compare them with a large group of websites not owned by corporate groups liable for the tax.

Working Paper

Salary Disclosures and the Value of Salary Confidentiality

With Stephanie Karol

Reject and resubmit, Journal of Public Economics

Abstract

How much do taxpayers value salary confidentiality? We leverage administrative data to estimate a revealed preference-based sufficient statistic for the willingness to pay for earnings confidentiality. We exploit the fact that nonprofits are required to publicly disclose the names and salaries of certain employees whose taxable earnings exceed $100,000. We find that taxpayers who bunch below the threshold reduce their pre-tax income by up to $3,076 to avoid disclosure. Employees whose earnings have recently been disclosed are also more likely to separate from their employers. We argue that this is indicative of an employee demand for privacy.

Working Paper

External Validity in Empirical Public Finance: With an Application to Taxable Income Elasticities

With Meng Hsuan Hsieh and Joel Slemrod

R&R, under review at Journal of Public Economics

Abstract

We propose a three-step empirical Bayes framework, grounded in statistical decision theory, to combine evidence across countries. The framework models cross-country heterogeneity using structural determinants of tax systems and determines how much each country’s estimate should draw on external evidence. Countries with less precise local estimates receive more weight on cross-country evidence. Applying the framework to 27 OECD countries, we show how estimates of taxable income elasticities can incorporate evidence from other countries and outline how to predict behavioral responses in countries that have not adopted a given tax.

Working Paper

The Value of Tax Privacy: Evidence from an Online Survey of U.S. Adults

Abstract

I estimate the perceived private costs of tax return disclosure in the United States using hypothetical willingness-to-pay and willingness-to-accept exercises in an online survey of U.S. taxpayers. The median willingness to pay for tax privacy is $50, while the mean is $257, reflecting a highly skewed distribution. Approximately 30 percent of respondents report that the personal benefits of tax return disclosure outweigh its risks. Privacy valuations depend substantially on how they are elicited: median willingness to accept compensation for disclosure is four times median willingness to pay to prevent it. These findings inform the trade-off between the private costs of losing tax privacy and the social benefits of tax transparency.
